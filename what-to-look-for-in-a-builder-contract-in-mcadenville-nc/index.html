Scope of Work and Custom Build Specifications

A builder contract should clearly define the scope of work so there is no ambiguity about what the builder is responsible for delivering. In McAdenville, where custom and semi-custom homes may range from traditional Southern styles to modern luxury designs, the contract must spell out construction standards, materials, and included features in detail.

The scope of work section outlines what is being built, how it will be built, and which standards apply. Vague language such as builder grade or industry standard can create misunderstandings later. Buyers should look for specificity around square footage, ceiling heights, exterior materials, interior finishes, and mechanical systems. The more detailed the scope, the fewer disputes arise during construction.

In luxury home construction, the scope of work often incorporates architectural plans, engineering documents, and design selections. These documents should be referenced directly in the contract and attached as exhibits whenever possible. If changes occur later, the contract should explain how updated plans are approved and incorporated.

Key elements to confirm in the scope of work include:

  • Detailed architectural and floor plans referenced by date and version
  • Specifications for exterior materials such as brick, siding, roofing, and windows
  • Interior finish details including flooring, cabinetry, countertops, and trim
  • Mechanical systems such as HVAC, plumbing, and electrical capacity
  • Energy efficiency standards and building code compliance

McAdenville falls under Gaston County building regulations, and the builder should confirm compliance with local codes, permitting requirements, and inspection processes. Ally Bohanan Real Estate often encourages buyers to review the scope of work with both their builder and a qualified third-party inspector or attorney before finalizing a contract.

For buyers relocating from outside North Carolina, understanding regional construction norms is especially important. Materials, climate considerations, and energy standards in the greater Charlotte region may differ from other parts of the country. A clear scope of work helps ensure the finished home aligns with expectations rather than assumptions.

Pricing Structure, Allowances, and Payment Terms

The pricing structure within a builder contract determines not only how much you pay, but when and under what conditions. In McAdenville, builder contracts may be structured as fixed-price, cost-plus, or hybrid agreements, each with different levels of risk and flexibility for the buyer.

A fixed-price contract offers predictability, as the total cost is set upfront based on agreed specifications. Cost-plus contracts, more common in high-end custom builds, involve paying the actual construction costs plus a builder fee. Hybrid contracts combine elements of both. Understanding which structure applies is essential before committing.

Allowances are another critical component. These are placeholder dollar amounts for items not fully selected at the time of contract signing, such as lighting, appliances, or tile. While allowances can provide flexibility, they also introduce potential cost overruns if the allowance is unrealistically low.

Important pricing and payment terms to review include:

  • Total contract price and whether it is fixed or adjustable
  • Allowance categories, dollar amounts, and adjustment process
  • Payment schedule tied to construction milestones rather than dates
  • Deposit requirements and conditions for refund or forfeiture
  • Responsibility for cost increases related to material shortages or tariffs

Buyers should also review how payments are triggered. Milestone-based payments tied to completed phases such as foundation, framing, and final completion provide more protection than arbitrary calendar-based payments. Documentation requirements for each draw should be clearly stated.

Construction Timeline and Delay Protection Clauses

A realistic construction timeline is one of the most important components of a builder contract. In McAdenville and nearby markets, timelines can be influenced by permitting, weather, labor availability, and material supply chains. A well-drafted contract acknowledges these variables while still holding the builder accountable.

The contract should specify an estimated start date and substantial completion date. It should also define what constitutes completion, such as receipt of a certificate of occupancy. Without clear definitions, buyers may find themselves in limbo, paying carrying costs while waiting for final delivery.

Delay clauses deserve close attention. Builders often include force majeure provisions that excuse delays caused by events beyond their control. While reasonable, these clauses should not be so broad that they eliminate accountability for preventable delays or poor project management.

Timeline-related items to review include:

  • Estimated construction start and completion dates
  • Definition of substantial completion and final completion
  • Permitted delay causes and documentation requirements
  • Buyer remedies if delays exceed a specified threshold
  • Temporary housing or rate lock considerations

Some contracts include liquidated damages or credits if delays exceed a certain timeframe, while others limit buyer remedies to contract termination. Understanding these provisions is especially important for buyers coordinating a home sale, relocation, or mortgage rate lock.

Change Orders, Upgrades, and Cost Transparency

Even with a detailed scope of work, changes are common during new construction. Change orders allow buyers to modify materials, layouts, or features after construction has begun. The builder contract should outline a clear and fair change order process.

A well-structured change order clause explains how changes are requested, approved, priced, and scheduled. It should require written approval from both parties before work proceeds. Verbal agreements or informal emails can lead to disputes over cost and responsibility.

Cost transparency is critical when it comes to upgrades. Buyers should understand whether pricing reflects retail costs, builder discounts, or markups. The contract should also specify how change orders impact the construction timeline.

Key change order considerations include:

  • Written change order requirement with signed approval
  • Pricing methodology for labor and materials
  • Impact of changes on completion timeline
  • Payment timing for approved upgrades
  • Limits on changes after certain construction phases

In luxury builds, upgrades often involve design-forward selections such as custom cabinetry, specialty lighting, or high-end appliances. Without clear pricing and documentation, these upgrades can significantly inflate the final cost.

Warranties, Inspections, and Quality Standards

Warranties and inspection rights protect buyers long after construction is complete. In North Carolina, builders typically provide a one-year workmanship warranty, with longer coverage for structural components. However, warranty terms vary, and the builder contract determines what is covered and how claims are handled.

The contract should clearly outline warranty durations, exclusions, and the process for submitting claims. Buyers should be cautious of clauses that limit remedies or require arbitration without adequate protections.

Inspection rights are equally important. Buyers should have the right to conduct independent inspections at key stages of construction, including pre-drywall and final walkthrough. These inspections help identify issues early when they are easier and less expensive to correct.

Quality and warranty items to review include:

  • Workmanship, systems, and structural warranty terms
  • Exclusions for normal wear, materials, or third-party products
  • Inspection rights during and after construction
  • Correction timelines for identified deficiencies
  • Final walkthrough and punch list procedures

Some builders require the use of their preferred inspectors or limit inspection timing. Buyers should evaluate whether these restrictions align with their comfort level and risk tolerance.

Local Disclosures and Dispute Resolution Options

Builder contracts in McAdenville should include disclosures specific to North Carolina and Gaston County. These may involve soil conditions, zoning restrictions, homeowners association requirements, and environmental considerations. Buyers should review these disclosures carefully and seek clarification when needed.

Dispute resolution clauses dictate how conflicts are handled if issues arise. Many builder contracts require mediation or arbitration rather than court litigation. While alternative dispute resolution can be efficient, buyers should understand the costs, limitations, and potential impact on their rights.

Important local and legal considerations include:

  • Required North Carolina property and construction disclosures
  • HOA documents and architectural review requirements
  • Zoning and setback compliance
  • Mediation, arbitration, or litigation procedures
  • Attorney review and governing law provisions

Buyers new to the area may not be familiar with local regulations or customary practices. Reviewing these sections with a qualified real estate attorney can provide valuable clarity and risk mitigation.
