Home Staging Tips to Sell Faster and for a Better Price

A room-by-room staging playbook for sellers in Gaston County and the Charlotte area, plus when virtual staging makes sense.

By Ally BohananPublished June 24, 20264 min read

The short answer

Home staging helps you sell faster and for a better price by making rooms look larger, brighter and easy to live in. Declutter and deep clean first, then arrange furniture for clear flow, give each room one purpose, use neutral colors and layered lighting, and focus on the living room, kitchen, primary bedroom and baths.

Key takeaways

  1. Declutter and deep clean first; no amount of decor fixes a crowded, dusty room.
  2. Give every room one clear purpose and pull furniture back to open walkways.
  3. The living room, kitchen, primary bedroom and baths matter most to buyers.
  4. Neutral walls and layered lighting make rooms look larger in photos and in person.
  5. Virtual staging can help vacant listings online, but label the photos clearly.

Most buyers see your home online before they ever walk in, and the photos decide whether they book a showing. Staging is how you make those photos, and the showings that follow, work in your favor. It isn't decorating. Decorating reflects your taste; staging is designed to appeal to as many buyers as possible so they can picture their own life in the house.

National research backs the idea. In NAR's Profile of Home Staging, buyers' agents report that staging makes it easier for buyers to visualize a property as their future home.

Where should you start?

Declutter

Clutter makes rooms look small and distracts buyers from the house itself. Pack up as if you've already moved:

  • Living areas: extra furniture, collections, stacks of books and most framed photos
  • Kitchen: everything but one or two items off the counters; thin out the pantry
  • Bedrooms: extra chairs, laundry, items under the bed; half-empty closets
  • Bathrooms: toiletries off counters and out of showers

For a full plan, see how to declutter before listing.

Deep clean

A spotless home reads as well maintained. Clean windows inside and out, baseboards, cabinet fronts, light fixtures, vents, appliances, grout and floors. Address odors at the source rather than covering them up.

Fix the small things

Dripping faucets, burned-out bulbs, chipped paint and sticking doors make buyers wonder what else has been neglected. Fix them before photos.

How should you arrange furniture?

  • Open the walkways. Buyers should be able to walk through each room without turning sideways.
  • Pull furniture off the walls where space allows, and group seating for conversation.
  • Give every room one purpose. A bonus room that's half office, half gym reads as storage. Pick one: office, guest room, dining room or family room.
  • Remove about a third of what's there in most rooms. It almost always looks better.

What colors and lighting work best?

Neutral walls such as soft whites, warm greiges and light taupes make rooms feel bigger and let buyers imagine their own furniture. If a room is a bold color, repainting is one of the most effective staging moves you can make.

For lighting, open every blind and curtain, use the same warm bulb color throughout and add lamps to dark corners. A mix of ceiling lights, table lamps and floor lamps makes rooms feel warm instead of flat.

How do you stage the rooms that matter most?

KitchenClear counters, organized cabinets, a bowl of fruit or one plant, updated hardware if it's datedMagnets on the fridge, small appliances out, dish racks

Should you stage for a specific buyer?

Think about who's most likely to buy your home and show them what they're looking for:

  • Families: a functional mudroom or drop zone, a play area, storage
  • Remote workers: a quiet, clearly staged home office
  • Downsizers: main-level living, low-maintenance features, easy access

Your agent can tell you who's been buying in your neighborhood and price range.

Is virtual staging a good idea?

Traditional stagingVirtual staging
How it worksReal furniture and decor in the homeFurniture added digitally to listing photos
Best forOccupied or vacant homes where showings matter mostVacant homes, especially for online marketing
StrengthsBuyers experience the space in personLower cost and fast to produce
Watch out forCost and schedulingBuyers may be disappointed by an empty home; photos must be clearly labeled and shouldn't hide defects

Many sellers combine the two: stage the main living areas in person and use virtual staging for secondary rooms.

What staging mistakes should you avoid?

  • Leaving too many personal items and photos out
  • Covering up repairs instead of making them
  • Overcrowding rooms with furniture or decor
  • Ignoring the yard, porch and patio
  • Letting the home slip between showings

How do you get ready for listing photos?

  1. Turn on every light and open every blind evenly.
  2. Make beds and smooth towels.
  3. Clear counters, sinks and showers.
  4. Move cars, trash cans and hoses out of exterior shots.
  5. Put pets, pet bowls and litter boxes out of sight.

Tip: Look at each room through your phone's camera before the photographer arrives. Clutter and crooked furniture are much easier to spot on a screen.

Frequently asked questions

How much does staging cost?

It depends on the home's size, whether it's vacant and whether you use rented furniture or work with what you have. A consultation with a stager or your agent is the best way to get a realistic estimate.

Should I stage if I'm still living in the house?

Yes. Occupied staging mostly means decluttering, rearranging what you own and adding a few fresh accessories. It's usually less expensive than staging a vacant home.

Do I need to stage every room?

No. Focus on the living room, kitchen, primary bedroom, main bath and any outdoor living space. Secondary bedrooms can simply be clean, neat and clearly furnished.

How long should staging stay in place?

Keep it in place until you're under contract and through the riskiest part of due diligence, since some buyers schedule second visits.
